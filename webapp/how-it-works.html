How NeuroScan turns a webcam into
a stroke-screening tool.

Most camera-based screeners fail when they need to work the most — during an active stroke. Here is the engineering decision that makes NeuroScan different, and the four-step pipeline that runs every test.

The clinical problem

Why static asymmetry tests fail.

The baseline contamination problem.

Most face-tracking stroke detectors compare the left and right side of a person's resting face. They look for a tilt — a drooping mouth corner, a ptosed eyelid.

But here is the clinical flaw. If a person is already mid-stroke when the test runs, their resting face is already asymmetric. The "normal" baseline is contaminated. Comparing a stroke face against a stroke baseline produces a near-zero delta — falsely reporting NORMAL at the moment accuracy matters most.

The same flaw affects stroke survivors with permanent facial asymmetry: a re-test two years later cannot tell whether new symptoms are appearing.

Static delta ≈ 0 in an active stroke
The fix

Measure movement, not stillness.

We measure how much each side of your face responds to a command. A stroke-affected side cannot move on demand — even when the person tries — and that failure is detectable regardless of what the resting baseline looks like.

From camera to verdict

The four-step pipeline.

Every frame your webcam captures runs through this exact pipeline, end-to-end, 30 times per second — all on your device.

01

Capture

Your webcam streams 640×480 frames at 30 FPS straight into the browser. Nothing ever leaves your device.

02

Detect

Google MediaPipe locates 468 anatomical landmarks — mouth corners, eyelids, brow, nose tip — on every frame.

03

Measure

During each guided action we record how far each side of your face moves and compute the left-right difference.

04

Score

A weighted asymmetry score is classified against clinically informed thresholds and shown with full charts.
